Light and shadowA generic beam as a flashlight: what it lights up, where shadows fall, and a bottom that isn't flat

The flashlight picture

Think of the transducer as a flashlight held just under the surface in a dark room, and the screen as a photo taken by a camera that only knows how far away each lit thing is, not in which direction.

  • Only what the light reaches answers. A shadow on the screen is not something dark on the bottom; it is a place no sound reached, so nothing came back from there.
  • Shadows point away from the light. The lower the light hits (farther out, or in shallower water), the longer the shadow behind anything that sticks up.
  • A fish in midwater casts its shadow farther out on the bottom. The fish is drawn where its echo is; its shadow is drawn farther out. The gap between them grows with how high the fish is above the bottom.
  • Slopes that face the light are brighter, slopes that face away are darker, and behind an edge that drops away steeper than the light comes in, there is a black band with nothing in it.
  • Shine it straight down and the shadows hide under things. That is why DownScan and 2D show few shadows, and SideScan, with its light coming in low, shows long ones.
  • Everything is placed by distance from the light. Two things at the same distance land on the same spot on the screen, even if one is up in the water and the other on the bottom.

Every other calculation on this page assumes a flat bottom. The card below lets you change the bottom and see what changes.

A generic beam, not a specific transducer: the low one lights from 6° to 60° below horizontal, the steep one from 55° straight down, both out to 5 × the depth. The beam slice

ActiveTarget sends a fan that is wide in one direction and thin in the other. The screen shows everything inside that thin slice as if it were flat. Anything outside the slice isn't shown at all.

Seen from above in Forward view: the slice gets thicker with distance, 2 × distance × tan(thickness ÷ 2). A fish a few meters to the side of your aim can be outside it close in, but inside it far out.

Where a target is drawn in Forward and Down

Forward and Down draw each echo at its true direction within the fan, so inside the slice the picture is to scale. A target off to the side of the slice is a little farther away than it looks, so it is drawn a little too far out along its line.

The mathThe formulas behind every section
SideScan

x = distance on the screen (slant), d = depth under the transducer, valid for x ≥ d. The true horizontal distance is √(x² − d²).

Solid error (screen as is)
x − √(x² − d²)
Dashed error (screen − depth)
√(x² − d²) − (x − d)
Sum of the two errors
= d, for every x
Curves cross at
x = 1.25 d, both = d/2
Solid error far out
≈ d² / (2x)
Object height from its shadow
d · (h₂ − h₁) / h₂
Suspended object depth
d · x_obj / x_shadow
Its horizontal distance
x_obj · √(x_shadow² − d²) / x_shadow
Bottom covered each side
d / tan(e + w) to d / tan(e)

h₁, h₂ = true horizontal distances to where the shadow starts and ends. e = upper edge of the fan below horizontal, w = fan width.

Why the sum of the errors is constant: add the two formulas and the square roots cancel, leaving d. The two shortcuts, x and x − d, are always exactly d apart, and the true horizontal distance always lies between them. Whatever one overshoots, the other undershoots by d minus that amount.

Example, d = 10 m, x = 20 m: true horizontal √(400 − 100) = 17.3 m. Solid error 20 − 17.3 = 2.7 m, dashed error 17.3 − 10 = 7.3 m, and 2.7 + 7.3 = 10 m = d.

Assumptions. A flat bottom, a level and steady transducer, and sound travelling in a straight line at the speed your unit uses. Slopes, thermoclines and a wrong depth offset all shift the answers. Real beams fade toward their edges instead of stopping sharply.
